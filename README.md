## Preface

This article concludes the series Uncountability of Continuum. Although its character remains scientific rather than engineering-oriented, the work is still investigative in nature: instead of presenting a finished, self-contained solution, it traces a line of inquiry and invites the reader to follow the same path of reasoning.

In the course of this investigation we identified several foundational difficulties in the justification of modern set theories. These difficulties include formal errors of hasty generalization - ancient sophisms already recognized by Aristotle. One of them is the fallacy of accident: we introduce a particular class of objects (sets of ordered pairs) and interpret them as functional relations between other objects of the theory. We never prove, however, that only objects of this form can be regarded as functional relations. Consequently, within the same theory alternative functional relations remain possible; indeed, as Cantor himself demonstrated, objects of this kind are required for the construction of transfinite operations. A second difficulty is the fallacy of false dichotomy. The existential quantifier in the Axiom of Infinity asserts the existence of an inductively closed object, yet it imposes no semantic restriction on the space of alternatives in which that object is located. As a result there is, strictly speaking, no exclusive dichotomy "finite / inductively infinite"; other notions of infinity remain conceivable. Formally this means that classical first-order logic cannot be applied without reservation in any chain of inference that begins from the Axiom of Infinity. The negation of "inductively infinite" may simply mean "otherwise infinite", so that the double negation need not recover inductive infiniteness. In such situations one is forced to work in Johansson’s minimal logic, which makes no assumption about the number of alternatives - the same situation that arises in certain general toposes. It is not sufficient to reply that we accept the axioms of first-order logic together with those of ZFC or NBG. Inside pure first-order logic completeness holds and syntax is isomorphic to semantics; once the set-theoretic axioms are added, this isomorphism disappears. Incompleteness permits statements that are true in a model yet unprovable in the formal system. Consequently the membership symbol itself may receive different interpretations according to the ambient notion of infinity. Syntactically the theory may then appear inconsistent, while semantically the contradictory statements belong to distinct model syntaxes and are therefore unrelated. Inference chains rooted in different conceptions of infinity never intersect. A further, characteristically mathematical error is the reification fallacy: algebraic structures defined on infinite sets are treated as if they were themselves sets. This is aggravated by the Axiom of Infinity in its classical formulation, which lacks stratification, unambiguity, purity and independence. When Norman Wildberger remarks that modern set theory is "either a religion or a joke", he is pointing precisely to this cluster of problems.

What is most striking is that none of these difficulties is fatal. Each of them can be neutralized by explicit formal stipulations. One need only declare at the outset that one is seeking solutions of the axiom system that, in addition, satisfy the requirements of unique functional relations and of a binary alternative (finite / inductively infinite). A restricted existential quantifier can be introduced that limits the space of alternatives, and one may agree to work only inside the fragment of the theory generated by the chosen definition of function. The reification problem and the precise wording of the Axiom of Infinity can even be left at the meta-theoretic level; they complicate the picture but do not destroy it. The decisive obstacle lies elsewhere: a confusion between internal and external notions of function that infects the core arguments of the theory. The secondary fallacies merely obscure this central conceptual error and make critical examination more difficult. As long as the fallacies remain philosophical rather than formal, the theory can still be given a philosophical justification. The moment they are admitted into the formal apparatus, alternative solutions of the same axiom system become visible, and the classical dichotomy proves to be only one - and not the most coherent - among them. The very existence of coherent alternatives is what confers scientific legitimacy upon those alternatives. Informal, ideological defence of the standard framework has therefore the effect of marginalizing other approaches and of concealing the original conceptual mistake. 

The practical consequences of this concealment have been severe. Wilfrid Hodges, in his well-known essay "An Editor Recalls Some Hopeless Papers", records that a single journal received dozens of manuscripts purporting to refute Cantor’s arguments during his editorship alone. The total number of authors seeking an alternative treatment of infinity is therefore certainly in the thousands. A clear social demand exists for a mathematical language that treats other forms of infinity as primary objects. Academic mathematics, however, systematically declines to meet that demand. The social institution charged with the development of mathematical language has ceased to perform its primary function; the phenomenon is one of institutional dysfunction, marking the end of a period of normal science and the onset of a parasitic phase in which the institution consumes resources in order to maintain an internal orthodoxy. Hodges’s own justification of editorial rejection is instructive. He objects that most authors invent private notions of the natural numbers, whereas "there is a common definition". The appeal already has the form of an argument from authority; it would be legitimate only if the standard theory supplied an unambiguous definition. It does not. The classic pattern of ideological defence by sophism is thereby reproduced. More seriously, the mainstream framework denies dissenting authors access to the principal channels of scholarly exchange. The formation of a community capable of elaborating a new conceptual language is blocked, and with it the possibility of replicating alternative ideas. In this respect institutional mathematics practises a form of ideocide - the systematic destruction of alternative replicating patterns of thought. The parallel with the physical destruction of dissenting communities (genocide) is not merely rhetorical. 

Against this background artificial intelligence constitutes a genuine bifurcation point for the further development of mathematical culture. If the limits of purely statistical expert systems are not recognized, we risk an ideological singularity rather than technological singularity: systems trained exclusively on the present conceptual framework will reproduce its fallacies and will suppress alternatives with even greater efficiency than human gate-keepers. A medieval AI trained on the texts of its own age would have been an exceptionally effective instrument for the detection and elimination of heresy. At the same time, AI possesses the capacity to complete the long historical project of engineering mathematics that began with the Prussian educational reforms of the nineteenth century. By absorbing the bulk of routine technical labour it can return mathematics to its original scientific status - the investigation of the functional rules of human thought. Which of these two paths is taken will decide whether the present crisis ends in another civilizational contraction or opens onto a new technological and conceptual paradigm.

## Abstract

The paper is devoted to a retrospective investigation into the crisis in the foundations of mathematics associated with the interpretation of the concept of infinity. An analysis of classical proofs of the uncountability of the set of real numbers, carried out in previous works of the series, revealed their substantial dependence on initial ontological and methodological conceptions of the morphology of the infinite. Under the assumption of a universal total structure that enables the comparison of infinite objects, these proofs remain valid; under the assumption of a fundamental homogeneity of all infinite objects, they prove internally inconsistent due to a conflation of referent positions. A detailed examination of existing axiomatic set theories (ZFC, NBG, and their variants) uncovers fundamental problems: fallacies of hasty generalization (accident and false dichotomy) in proving reflexivity, the minimality of ω, and the equality ⋃ ω = ω; architectural defects associated with an ill-formed axiom of infinity (lack of stratification, ambiguity, and lack of purity and independence); the fallacy of reification; and, ultimately, the persistence of conditional consistency, whose nature remains unclear—either the explicit and implicit assumptions of the modern interpretation genuinely do not contradict one another, or censorship axioms isolate a consistent fragment of a contradictory theory. The modern interpretation effectively negates Cantor’s original theorem for ordered sets by extending internal (cardinal) induction to limit objects and leading to equalities such as ω = 2^ω, which conflicts with the axiom of continuity and blocks natural mechanisms for strengthening predicativity to resist semantic loops. The authors identify five key mathematical paradigms—formalist, intuitionist, logicist, constructivist, and structural—and analyze them across six criteria: truth, identity, expressibility of objects, logical closure, continuum, and metatheory. This analysis reveals significant differences in the assumptions defining the boundaries of applicability of each paradigm and explains why representatives of different schools often speak different languages even when discussing the same theorems, while at the same time showing that each of them is indispensable to mathematics.

The notions of referent positions, regions of regularity and singularity, and cardinal, ordinal, and indexal morphologies are introduced, serving as the basis for formulating a unified conceptual language for describing infinity that clarifies the ambiguity of traditional concepts (potential/actual infinity, dynamism/staticism, intensionality/extensionality). Through the lens of this conceptual apparatus, the paper traces the history of how the modern interpretation of set theory took shape—from Cantor’s original ontology, through simplified early-twentieth-century adaptations and the origins of ZFC/NBG, to von Neumann’s breakthrough contributions and the entrenchment of the dominant picture, accompanied by gradual dogmatization and institutional censorship of alternative approaches. A theoretical construction is proposed that unifies alternative conceptions of infinity with the modern interpretation into a consistent picture. Axioms of limitation and safe induction are introduced, along with a stratification of the membership relation and axioms of indexal and cardinal infinity, enabling proper switching of perspectives and restoring alignment with Cantor’s original ontology. The work demonstrates that many paradoxes of self-reference arise from conflating referent positions rather than from self-reference itself. In conclusion, key directions for further research are outlined: constructing conservative extensions (ZFCS, NBGS, ZFCL, NBGL) and synthesizing them with surreal numbers, Quine’s New Foundations, gross-set theory, and non-standard analysis; investigating the diversification of continuum annotation models and its consequences for measure theory; applying the apparatus of referent positions to semantic theorems (Gödel, Tarski, Kleene, Turing) and the problem of artificial intelligence; developing formal criteria for detecting object censorship in axiomatic systems; and integrating cognitive-mathematical and historical-philosophical methods to overcome institutional stagnation in the foundations of mathematics. The proposed unifying picture brings together a substantial number of disparate areas into a single consistent system and opens a path toward methodologically honest work with infinity.
